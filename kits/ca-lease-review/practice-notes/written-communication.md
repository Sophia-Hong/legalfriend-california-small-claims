# Putting it in writing

> DRAFT — AI-drafted for attorney review. Not for release until approved.

A friendly phone call or hallway chat is a fine way to get along with a landlord. But when something matters — a repair, a question about the lease, a notice, a move-out date — a short written message gives both sides the same record. This note covers how to write those messages and keep track of them. It is general information, not advice about any specific situation.

## Why writing helps both sides

Memories differ. A dated message shows what was asked, when, and the answer. It helps a landlord plan repairs and a renter show they raised an issue. Several California rules also turn on notice or timing, so a record of dates is useful.

## A simple format

Keep each message short and specific:

1. **What** — one topic per message. "The kitchen sink is leaking under the cabinet."
2. **Where** — unit number and room.
3. **When** — when you noticed it, or the date you're asking about.
4. **What you're asking** — "Could someone take a look this week?" or "Could you confirm…?"
5. **Access** — times that work for you, and how to reach you.

Attach a photo when it helps. Leave out guesses about motives and stay with the facts.

## Repairs

California rental housing must meet basic habitability standards, and the lease cannot waive that duty. [F:habitability] LegalFriend's standard wording on this point is: "Despite any contradicting clause, your landlord is still required to maintain heating, plumbing, and a safe structure. Document any issues and ask for repairs in writing."

California also has a "repair and deduct" rule, but it applies only after notice and a reasonable time to fix a habitability problem, with limits on amount and frequency, and its conditions are strict. [F:repair-and-deduct] A written repair request is the usual starting point for any next step, and this kit does not recommend using that rule in any specific case.

## Entry

Except in an emergency, a landlord may enter only for specified reasons, during normal business hours, after reasonable written notice; 24 hours is presumed reasonable. [F:entry-notice] If entry times don't work for you, a polite written reply suggesting other times keeps things clear.

## Rent increases and notices to end

For a month-to-month tenancy, a rent increase of 10% or less needs at least 30 days' written notice, and more than 10% needs at least 90 days. [F:rent-increase-notice] To end a month-to-month tenancy, a tenant generally gives 30 days' notice. [F:termination-notice-mtm] Write down the date any notice is given or received.

## Before move-out

A tenant may request an initial inspection before moving out, no earlier than two weeks before the tenancy ends. [F:deposit-pre-moveout-inspection] Make the request in writing so the date is clear.

## Raising concerns

A landlord may not retaliate — for example by raising rent, reducing services, or ending the tenancy — because a tenant exercised legal rights. [F:retaliation] Calm, written requests are part of a normal tenancy.

## Keeping a log

Keep one simple log with columns for date, method (email, portal, letter), topic, what was asked, and the reply. Save emails and portal messages as PDFs or screenshots, and keep copies of letters. Keep photos with the message they relate to.

## Common pitfalls

- Reporting a repair only by phone, with no written follow-up. [F:habitability]
- Putting several unrelated topics in one long message.
- Writing while upset; wait and reread before sending.
- Not keeping a copy of what was sent.
- Treating an informal reply as a change to the lease instead of asking for a signed addendum.
- Missing a notice date because it was never written down. [F:rent-increase-notice]
