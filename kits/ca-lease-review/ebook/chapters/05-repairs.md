# Repairs and Habitability

> DRAFT — AI-drafted from sources/facts.yaml for attorney review. Not for release until approved.

**In this chapter:** Most leases have a section on maintenance and repairs. This chapter shows how to read that section, explains the basic standards every California rental must meet, and walks through asking for repairs in writing. It also covers two protections, repair-and-deduct and protection from retaliation, and why you should read the official source before you rely on either one.

## Who fixes what: reading the maintenance section

Leases split repair duties in many different ways. Some use one short sentence. Others use a long list or a chart. Look for headings such as "Maintenance," "Repairs," "Condition of Premises," "Appliances," or "Landscaping." Repair terms can also show up in the rules, utilities, or move-in sections.

As you read, sort each item into one of three groups:

- **Landlord handles.** For example, "Landlord will maintain the heating system."
- **Tenant handles.** For example, replacing light bulbs, changing air filters, or yard care.
- **Not mentioned.** Items the lease does not address, such as a particular appliance.

Write down the clause number next to each item. Note how the lease says to report problems (a form, an email address, an online portal, or a phone number) and whether it says reports must be "in writing." When you ask your AI to explain this section, ask it to quote the exact clause instead of summarizing from memory. If a clause is unclear, add it to your list of questions for the landlord or property manager.

> **LegalFriend practice note** — Before you share your lease with an AI provider, you may want to redact Social Security numbers, bank details, and signatures. The AI provider's terms govern what happens to what you share.

## Habitability: the basic standards

"Habitability" means a home is fit to live in. California rental housing must meet basic habitability standards. These include weatherproofing, working plumbing, hot and cold water, heating, electrical systems, safe floors and stairways, and freedom from rodents and vermin [F:habitability]. The full list is in the statute [S:civ-1941-1].

This duty cannot be waived by the lease [F:habitability]. A lease clause that has the tenant give up the right to a habitable home is void. The same is true of a clause that has the tenant give up the right to hold the landlord responsible for the landlord's own negligence [F:waivers-void]. "Void" means the clause has no legal effect. See the statute for the exact wording [S:civ-1953].

So if a lease says something like "Tenant accepts the unit as-is and is responsible for all repairs," that clause is worth a closer look. As applied to the basic habitability standards, it may not be enforceable as written.

> **LegalFriend practice note** — LegalFriend says: "Despite any contradicting clause, your landlord is still required to maintain heating, plumbing, and a safe structure. Document any issues and ask for repairs in writing." [F:habitability]

This does not mean a lease can never assign any upkeep to the tenant. Many leases ask tenants to handle small items. This kit does not cover who pays for a particular repair or what happens when a tenant or guest causes damage. For those questions, check the official guide [S:dre-california-tenants].

## Asking for repairs in writing

A written request creates a record of what you reported and when. That record helps both sides. It shows the landlord exactly what needs attention. It also gives you a clear history if questions come up later, including at move-out (Chapter 8).

A good repair request is short, polite, and factual. Include:

- The date, your name, and the unit address.
- What the problem is, where it is, and when you first noticed it.
- How it affects daily use of the home (for example, "no hot water in the kitchen or bathroom").
- Photos, if they help show the problem.
- A request to schedule the repair, and the best way to reach you to arrange access (see Chapter 6).

Send the request the way your lease says to report problems. If the lease does not say, use a method that leaves a record, such as email or a portal message. Keep a copy. Log every follow-up: the date, who you spoke with, and what was said. If you talk by phone, send a short email afterward that sums up the call.

> **LegalFriend practice note** — Your AI can help you draft a calm, factual repair request. Ask it to keep a neutral tone, stick to facts you can show, and leave out guesses about why the problem happened.

## Repair-and-deduct: a strict, limited option

California law has a narrow remedy called "repair and deduct." After giving the landlord notice and a reasonable time to fix a habitability problem, a tenant may in some cases pay for the repair and deduct the cost from rent. The deduction is limited to one month's rent, it can be used no more than twice in 12 months, and the conditions are strict [F:repair-and-deduct].

This kit does not list those conditions. Before you rely on this option, read the statute and an official guide [S:civ-1942] [S:dre-california-tenants]. Because the conditions are strict, many renters talk with a local legal aid office or an attorney before using it.

## Protection from retaliation

A landlord may not retaliate against a tenant for using legal rights, such as complaining to a government agency. Examples of retaliation include raising the rent, reducing services, or ending the tenancy [F:retaliation]. Retaliation is presumed for actions taken within 180 days after certain protected acts [F:retaliation].

Which acts are protected, and how the presumption works, are set out in the statute [S:civ-1942-5]. The Attorney General's landlord-tenant page is another official starting point [S:oag-landlord-tenant]. Your dated repair requests and notes can help show the order in which things happened.
