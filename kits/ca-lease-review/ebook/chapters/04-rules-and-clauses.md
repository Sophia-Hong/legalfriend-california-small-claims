# Rules, Restrictions, and Clauses Worth a Closer Look

> DRAFT — AI-drafted from sources/facts.yaml for attorney review. Not for release until approved.

**In this chapter:** Every lease has house rules, and most are routine. This chapter shows how to read rules about pets, guests, subletting, and use of the home. It then covers a short list of clause types that are worth a closer look under California law, with what the law says about each.

## Reading the house rules

House rules help a building run smoothly for everyone. For each rule, ask: What exactly is allowed? What needs permission? What happens if the rule is not followed?

**Pets.** Look for which animals are allowed, how many, any size or breed limits, and whether a pet addendum is required. Note any pet deposit or monthly pet rent. Pet deposits generally count toward the overall deposit limit [F:deposit-what-counts]. This kit does not cover assistance animals. If that applies to you, check the official guidance [S:oag-landlord-tenant].

**Guests.** Many leases limit how long a guest may stay before they must be added to the lease. Check the number of days and whether you must tell the landlord in advance.

**Subletting and assignment.** "Subletting" means renting part or all of your unit to someone else while you remain on the lease. "Assignment" means transferring your lease to someone else. Leases often require written permission for either. Note what the lease says and how to ask.

**Use of the home.** Look for rules on home businesses, short-term rentals, smoking, quiet hours, parking, storage, and changes such as painting. If the lease mentions community or HOA rules, ask for a copy.

Rules can also come from your city or county. Many California cities and counties have their own tenant protection rules that can be stricter than state law, and the rules for the property's address also apply [F:local-rules]. Check your city's rules [S:civ-1947-12].

> **LegalFriend practice note** — If a rule matters to your daily life, such as a pet, a partner who stays over often, or working from home, ask about it in writing before you sign. A clear answer now can prevent a misunderstanding later.

## Clauses worth a closer look

California law limits how some common clauses work. If your lease has a clause like one of these, it may not be enforceable as written. That depends on the exact wording and the facts, so treat these as points to ask about, not conclusions about your lease. The kit's `prompts/clause-check.md` prompt can help you find them.

### Waivers of basic tenant rights

A "waiver" is a clause where one side gives up a right. A lease clause that has the tenant give up rights to a habitable home, to proper entry notice, to the deposit protections, or to hold the landlord responsible for the landlord's own negligence is void [F:waivers-void]. See the official text [S:civ-1953].

Look for phrases like "tenant waives," "as is," "landlord is not responsible for any damage," or "tenant gives up any claim."

**Habitability.** California rental housing must meet basic habitability standards — including weatherproofing, working plumbing, hot and cold water, heating, electrical systems, safe floors and stairways, and freedom from rodents and vermin — and this duty cannot be waived by the lease [F:habitability]. See the official text [S:civ-1941-1] and Chapter 5.

> **LegalFriend practice note** — LegalFriend's former Lease Review service used this attorney-approved line for repair clauses: "Despite any contradicting clause, your landlord is still required to maintain heating, plumbing, and a safe structure. Document any issues and ask for repairs in writing." [F:habitability]

**Negligence.** "Negligence" means failing to use reasonable care. A clause that tries to release the landlord from responsibility for the landlord's own negligence falls in the category described above [F:waivers-void].

> **LegalFriend practice note** — For liability waivers, the former Lease Review service used this attorney-approved line: "Despite this clause, landlords remain liable for their negligence and must provide habitable housing." [F:waivers-void]

**Entry.** Except in an emergency, a landlord may enter only for specified reasons, during normal business hours, after reasonable written notice — 24 hours is presumed reasonable (six days if the notice is mailed) [F:entry-notice]. A clause allowing entry "at any time" or "without notice" is worth a closer look. See the official text [S:civ-1954] and Chapter 6.

> **LegalFriend practice note** — For entry clauses, the former Lease Review service used this attorney-approved line: "California requires reasonable notice (commonly 24+ hours) for non-emergency entry" [F:entry-notice]

**Deposit.** A clause that has the tenant give up deposit protections is also in the void category [F:waivers-void]. Chapter 3 covers deposits in more detail.

### Jury trial waivers

Some leases say both sides give up the right to a jury trial if a dispute happens. A pre-dispute waiver of the right to a jury trial in a contract such as a lease is unenforceable in California [F:jury-waiver]. See the court decision [S:case-grafton-2005].

> **LegalFriend practice note** — For jury waivers, the former Lease Review service used this attorney-approved line: "Despite this clause, you can still request a jury trial. This waiver is invalid in CA." [F:jury-waiver]

### One-sided attorney's fees

Some leases say that if there is a dispute, the landlord can recover attorney's fees from the tenant. If a lease says only the landlord can recover attorney's fees in a dispute, California law makes that clause apply to both sides [F:attorney-fees-reciprocal]. See the official text [S:civ-1717].

### Automatic renewal

An "automatic renewal" clause extends the lease unless someone gives notice in time. An automatic renewal or extension clause in a residential lease is voidable by the tenant unless it appears in at least 8-point boldface type immediately above the tenant's signature [F:auto-renewal]. "Voidable" means the tenant may choose to treat the clause as not binding. See the official text [S:civ-1945-5].

Check where the clause appears and how it is printed, and put any notice deadline on your calendar. Chapter 7 covers renewal.
