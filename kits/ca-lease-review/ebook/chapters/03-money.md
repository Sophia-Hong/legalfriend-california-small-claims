# Money: Rent, Deposits, and Fees

> DRAFT — AI-drafted from sources/facts.yaml for attorney review. Not for release until approved.

**In this chapter:** Money terms are often the clearest part of a lease, and also the part most worth double-checking. This chapter explains what counts as a deposit in California, the general deposit limits, late fees, screening fees, and rent increases at a high level. Chapter 7 covers rent increases in more detail.

## Start with a money summary

Before looking at the law, list every dollar amount in your lease and its addenda. Include:

- Monthly rent, due date, and how to pay.
- Every deposit, by name and amount.
- Late fees, returned-payment fees, and any other fees.
- Utilities and services you pay (water, trash, internet, parking, storage).
- Move-in costs, such as first month's rent and any prorated rent.

The kit's `prompts/lease-extraction.md` prompt can build this list from your lease. Check its numbers against the lease yourself.

## What counts as a deposit

In California, "security" for a residential lease includes any payment, fee, deposit, or charge used to cover unpaid rent, damage, or cleaning. So security, pet, key, and cleaning deposits generally count together toward the limit [F:deposit-what-counts]. Application screening fees are treated separately [F:deposit-what-counts].

This means the name on a charge does not decide how it is treated. A "pet deposit" or "cleaning fee" that covers damage or cleaning is generally part of the deposit total.

> **LegalFriend practice note** — When LegalFriend's former Lease Review service explained deposits, it used this attorney-approved line: "All deposits combined (security + pet + other) must comply with CA limits: 1 month rent for most landlords, 2 months for small owners (≤2 properties, ≤4 units)" [F:deposit-what-counts]

## Deposit limits

**The general rule.** For most residential leases since July 1, 2024, a landlord may not demand or receive total security deposits of more than one month's rent [F:deposit-limit-general].

**Small landlords.** A small landlord may collect up to two months' rent as security. A "small landlord" here is generally a natural person (or an LLC or family trust made up of natural persons) who owns no more than two residential rental properties with no more than four units in total [F:deposit-limit-small-landlord].

**Service members.** The small-landlord exception does not apply when the tenant is a service member. In that case, the one-month limit applies [F:deposit-limit-servicemember].

You usually cannot tell from a lease alone whether a landlord is a "small landlord." If your total deposits are more than one month's rent, that is a good question to ask in writing. Check the official text for the exact rules [S:civ-1950-5].

**A simple check:**

1. Add up every deposit and deposit-like charge.
2. Compare the total to one month's rent.
3. If it is higher, note it as something to ask about.

## "Nonrefundable" deposits

A lease may not make a security deposit, or any part of it, nonrefundable [F:deposit-no-nonrefundable]. If your lease labels a deposit, or part of one, as "nonrefundable," that wording is worth a closer look. Since deposit-like charges generally count as security [F:deposit-what-counts], ask the landlord how the charge is used and whether it is part of the deposit.

## Late fees

A late fee must be a reasonable estimate of the landlord's actual costs from late payment. A fee set as a penalty may be unenforceable [F:late-fee-reasonable]. A late fee above about 5% of monthly rent is commonly treated as a signal to look more closely [F:late-fee-reasonable]. The official sources are the statute [S:civ-1671] and a court decision on late fees [S:case-orozco-2004].

To check: divide the late fee by your monthly rent. For example, a $60 late fee on $2,000 rent is 3% ($60 ÷ $2,000 = 0.03).

> **LegalFriend practice note** — For late fees above that level, LegalFriend's former Lease Review service used this attorney-approved line: "This late fee is over 5% of rent (California's common "reasonableness" benchmark)—if charged, demand proof of the landlord's actual costs before paying." [F:late-fee-reasonable] Before signing, a polite written question about how the fee was set is often the simplest step.

Also look for how a grace period works, when a fee starts, and whether any fee repeats daily.

## Application screening fees

An application screening fee is capped by state law at an amount adjusted each year for inflation, and the applicant is entitled to an itemized receipt [F:screening-fee]. Because the cap changes, this kit does not give a number. Check the current cap in the official source [S:civ-1950-6].

## Rent increases (a quick look)

Rent increases are covered in Chapter 7. Here are the basics:

- **Notice.** For a month-to-month tenancy, a rent increase of 10% or less requires at least 30 days' written notice. An increase of more than 10% requires at least 90 days' notice [F:rent-increase-notice]. See the official text [S:civ-827].
- **Statewide cap.** For many units covered by the Tenant Protection Act, rent increases over any 12-month period are limited to 5% plus the regional change in the cost of living, and never more than 10% [F:rent-cap]. Some housing is exempt, for example units built within the past 15 years and many single-family homes or condos when the lease contains the required exemption notice [F:rent-cap]. See the official text [S:civ-1947-12].
- **Local rules.** Many California cities and counties have their own rent stabilization or tenant protection rules that can be stricter than state law [F:local-rules]. Check your city's rules.

Whether a particular unit is covered or exempt depends on facts this kit cannot decide. If your lease includes an exemption notice, note it and ask about it.
