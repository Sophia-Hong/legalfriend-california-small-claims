# Before You Sign

> DRAFT — AI-drafted from sources/facts.yaml for attorney review. Not for release until approved.

**In this chapter:** A lease is easier to understand when you know how it is put together. This chapter shows the usual parts of a lease, a simple way to read it section by section, and how to ask questions in writing before you sign. It also covers your right to a translation in some cases and why local rules matter.

## How a lease is organized

Most residential leases follow a similar pattern, even when the headings differ. You will usually find:

- **Parties and property.** Who the landlord and tenants are, and the unit's address.
- **Term.** The start date, and either an end date (a fixed-term lease) or a month-to-month arrangement.
- **Rent and other charges.** The amount, due date, how to pay, and any fees.
- **Deposits.** How much is collected and what it is called.
- **Rules.** Pets, guests, parking, noise, smoking, and changes to the unit.
- **Maintenance and repairs.** Who handles what, and how to request repairs.
- **Entry.** When the landlord may come in.
- **Ending the lease.** Renewal, notice, and moving out.
- **Addenda.** Extra pages, such as a pet addendum, parking rules, or community rules. These are part of the deal too.
- **Signatures and initials.** Where each party signs, sometimes with initials on certain clauses.

An "addendum" is an added page that changes or adds to the main lease. Ask for every addendum before you sign, not after.

## Reading it section by section

A long lease is less overwhelming if you sort it into six topics. These match the six sections the kit's `prompts/lease-extraction.md` prompt uses.

1. **Money.** Rent, due date, late fees, utilities, and other charges. In California, deposits of all kinds (security, pet, key, cleaning) generally count together toward one limit [F:deposit-what-counts]. See Chapter 3.
2. **Rules.** What you can and cannot do in the home. See Chapter 4.
3. **Repairs.** Who fixes what. California rental housing must meet basic habitability standards, such as working plumbing, heat, and hot and cold water, and the lease cannot waive that duty [F:habitability]. See Chapter 5.
4. **Privacy.** When the landlord may enter. Except in an emergency, a landlord may enter only for specified reasons, during normal business hours, after reasonable written notice; 24 hours is presumed reasonable [F:entry-notice]. See Chapter 6.
5. **Ending.** How the lease ends or renews. For a month-to-month tenancy, a tenant generally gives 30 days' notice to end it [F:termination-notice-mtm]. See Chapter 7.
6. **Deposit.** How the deposit comes back. Within 21 days after you move out, the landlord must return the deposit or send an itemized statement of deductions [F:deposit-21-days].

For each section, write down three things: what the lease says, what is unclear, and what you want to ask.

> **LegalFriend practice note** — Read the lease once straight through without stopping. Then read it again, one section at a time, with a highlighter. The first pass gives you the big picture. The second pass is where questions come up.

## The right to a translation

If a lease is negotiated mainly in Spanish, Chinese, Tagalog, Vietnamese, or Korean, the landlord must give the tenant a translation in that language before signing [F:translation]. See the official text for details [S:civ-1632].

If the conversations about your lease happened in one of those languages, ask for the translation before you sign. Your AI can also help you understand a clause, but its translation is an aid, not a substitute.

## Ask questions in writing

Questions are normal. A good landlord or property manager expects them. Asking in writing helps both sides: it creates a clear record and reduces misunderstandings later.

Tips for written questions:

- Use email or a text message you can save.
- Keep each question short and specific. Quote the clause or give its number.
- Ask one thing per question, so each answer is clear.
- Stay polite and neutral. "Could you help me understand…" works well.
- If an answer changes what the lease says, ask whether the lease or an addendum will be updated to match.

The kit's `prompts/questions-for-landlord.md` prompt can turn your notes into a neutral list of questions.

> **LegalFriend practice note** — A verbal promise is hard to prove later. If the landlord agrees to something, such as fixing an item before move-in, ask to have it written into the lease or an addendum.

## Get a fully signed copy

Before you move in, make sure you have a complete copy of the lease, signed by everyone, with every addendum. Check that:

- All pages are there, in order.
- Blanks are filled in or crossed out.
- Any handwritten changes are initialed by both sides.
- The rent, deposit, and dates match what you agreed to.

Store a copy somewhere safe, along with your move-in record (see `prompts/move-in-record.md`).

## Local rules matter

State law is only part of the picture. Many California cities and counties have their own rent or tenant protection rules that can be stricter than state law, and the rules for the property's address also apply [F:local-rules]. Check your city's rules before you sign. The state law reference is here [S:civ-1947-12], and the Attorney General's landlord-tenant page has a general overview [S:oag-landlord-tenant].
