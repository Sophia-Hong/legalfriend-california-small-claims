# Ending, Renewing, and Rent Increases

> DRAFT — AI-drafted from sources/facts.yaml for attorney review. Not for release until approved.

**In this chapter:** Every lease ends or renews at some point, and rent can change along the way. This chapter explains the difference between fixed-term and month-to-month leases, what to look for in renewal clauses, the basic notice rules for ending a tenancy and raising rent, and what to know if you need to leave early.

## Fixed-term vs. month-to-month

A **fixed-term lease** runs for a set period, such as 12 months, with a start date and an end date. A **month-to-month tenancy** has no set end date. It continues from month to month until someone gives notice.

Find out which one you have. Look at the "Term" section near the start of the lease. Then look for what the lease says happens when the term ends. Some leases say the tenancy becomes month-to-month. Others say it renews for another term. Some say nothing. What happens when a lease is silent is not covered by this kit. Check the official guide [S:dre-california-tenants].

## Automatic renewal clauses

Some leases say they renew or extend automatically unless someone gives notice by a deadline. In a residential lease, an automatic renewal or extension clause is voidable by the tenant unless it appears in at least 8-point boldface type immediately above the tenant's signature [F:auto-renewal]. "Voidable" means the tenant may choose to treat the clause as not binding.

If your lease has a renewal clause, check where it appears and how it is printed. A renewal clause in regular type, or placed somewhere other than right above your signature, is worth a closer look [F:auto-renewal] [S:civ-1945-5]. Either way, write the renewal notice deadline on your calendar.

## Notice to end a month-to-month tenancy

To end a month-to-month tenancy, where ending it is otherwise allowed, a landlord generally must give 30 days' notice if the tenant has lived there less than a year. If the tenant has lived there a year or more, the landlord generally must give 60 days' notice. A tenant generally gives 30 days' notice [F:termination-notice-mtm]. See the statute for details and exceptions [S:civ-1946-1].

Your lease may also say how notice must be given, such as in writing or to a certain address. Follow it, and keep a copy of any notice you send or receive.

## Just cause

For many tenancies covered by the Tenant Protection Act, once the tenant has lived in the unit for 12 months, the landlord needs a "just cause" reason to end the tenancy. Some no-fault reasons require relocation assistance [F:just-cause]. "Just cause" means a reason the law accepts. "No-fault" means a reason that is not based on anything the tenant did.

This kit does not list the just-cause reasons or say whether your unit is covered. Check the official source [S:civ-1946-2].

## Rent increases

**Notice.** For a month-to-month tenancy, a rent increase of 10% or less requires at least 30 days' written notice. An increase of more than 10% requires at least 90 days' notice [F:rent-increase-notice] [S:civ-827].

**The statewide cap.** For many units covered by the Tenant Protection Act, rent increases over any 12-month period are limited to 5% plus the regional change in the cost of living, and never more than 10% [F:rent-cap]. Some housing is exempt. Examples include units built within the past 15 years, and many single-family homes or condos when the lease contains the required exemption notice [F:rent-cap].

This kit cannot tell you whether your unit is covered or exempt. If your lease includes a notice saying the unit is exempt, note the clause number and read the official source [S:civ-1947-12].

## Local rules

Many California cities and counties have their own rent stabilization or tenant protection ordinances. These can be stricter than state law, and the local rules for the property's address also apply [F:local-rules]. This kit does not cover any city or county's rules. Check your city's rules, and your county's if the property is outside city limits.

> **LegalFriend practice note** — Ask your AI to list every date and deadline in your lease: the start date, the end date, any renewal notice deadline, and any notice period for moving out. Put them on your calendar with a reminder a few weeks ahead.

## Leaving early

Sometimes plans change. If a tenant leaves before a fixed-term lease ends, the landlord must make reasonable efforts to re-rent the unit. The tenant is generally responsible only for rent losses that could not reasonably have been avoided [F:mitigation]. This duty is often called "mitigation," which means reducing the loss [S:civ-1951-2].

If your lease has an early termination fee or a "lease break" clause, note the amount and the clause number. Questions about how such a clause interacts with the re-rental duty are worth asking about.

Practical steps that help both sides:

- Tell the landlord in writing, as early as you can, that you need to leave early and your planned move-out date.
- Offer to help find a replacement tenant, and ask what screening process the landlord uses.
- Leave the unit clean and ready to show.
- Ask for any final amount in writing, with an itemized breakdown.

### Early termination for survivors of violence

A tenant who is a victim of domestic violence, sexual assault, stalking, or certain other crimes may end a lease early with written notice and qualifying documentation [F:dv-termination]. This kit does not list the documentation, deadlines, or how rent is handled after notice. Read the statute or an official guide before acting [S:civ-1946-7] [S:oag-landlord-tenant]. Local legal aid and victim services organizations can also help.
