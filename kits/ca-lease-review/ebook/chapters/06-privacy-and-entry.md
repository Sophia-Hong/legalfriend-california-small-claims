# Privacy and Landlord Entry

> DRAFT — AI-drafted from sources/facts.yaml for attorney review. Not for release until approved.

**In this chapter:** Your rental is your home, and the landlord also has reasons to come in, such as repairs and inspections. This chapter explains California's basic entry notice rule, how to read the entry clause in your lease, and practical ways to keep visits smooth for everyone.

## The entry notice rule

Except in an emergency, a landlord may enter a rental only for specified reasons, during normal business hours, and after reasonable written notice [F:entry-notice]. Twenty-four hours' notice is presumed reasonable. If the notice is mailed, six days is presumed reasonable [F:entry-notice].

The list of reasons a landlord may enter is in the statute, and this kit does not repeat it. This kit also does not cover how "reasonable" is judged in a particular situation. Check the official source [S:civ-1954].

> **LegalFriend practice note** — LegalFriend says: "California requires reasonable notice (commonly 24+ hours) for non-emergency entry" [F:entry-notice]

A lease clause that has the tenant give up the right to proper entry notice is void, which means it has no legal effect [F:waivers-void]. See the statute for the exact wording [S:civ-1953].

## Emergencies

The notice rule has an exception for emergencies [F:entry-notice]. This kit does not define what counts as an emergency. If that question comes up, check the official source [S:civ-1954].

Some planning ahead helps in a real emergency:

- Make sure the landlord or manager has a current phone number and email for you.
- Ask how they would contact you if they had to enter during an emergency, and how they would let you know afterward.
- Keep the landlord's emergency contact number somewhere easy to find.
- If you have pets, note that in writing so anyone entering knows to look out for them.

## Reading your lease's entry clause

Look for headings such as "Entry," "Access," "Right of Entry," "Inspections," or "Showings." Entry terms can also appear in the repairs or move-out sections. For each clause, write down:

- **Notice period.** How many hours or days of notice the lease says the landlord will give.
- **How notice is given.** Text, email, a note on the door, or mail.
- **Reasons listed.** Repairs, inspections, showings to new tenants or buyers, or anything else.
- **Hours.** Whether the lease names days or times for entry.
- **Keys and lockboxes.** Whether the lease mentions a lockbox, spare keys, or changing locks.
- **Broad language.** Phrases such as "at any time" or "with or without notice."

Then compare what you wrote down to the notice rule. A clause that allows non-emergency entry with less than the 24 hours' notice presumed reasonable, or without written notice, is worth a closer look [F:entry-notice]. It may not be enforceable as written [F:waivers-void]. For example, a clause that says "Landlord may enter with 12 hours' notice" is one to ask about.

When you ask your AI about this section, ask it to quote the exact clause, compare it to the facts in this kit, and list neutral questions you could ask the landlord. It should not decide whether your clause is enforceable. It can point out what is worth a closer look and why.

## Keeping communication in writing

Written notes protect both sides. They show what was asked, what was agreed, and when.

- **Keep every entry notice.** Save texts and emails. Take a photo of any note left on the door.
- **Reply in writing.** A short "Thanks, Tuesday at 10 a.m. works" is enough.
- **Keep an entry log.** For each visit, record the date, time, who came in, the reason given, and how much notice you received.
- **Follow up on calls.** If you agree to something by phone, send a short email to confirm it.

If you have a concern about the timing or number of visits, raise it calmly in writing. Describe what happened using your log, and suggest a fix, such as a regular time window for visits. This kit does not cover what rules apply to how often a landlord may enter. Check the official sources [S:civ-1954] [S:oag-landlord-tenant] [S:dre-california-tenants].

## Practical scheduling tips

Most entry questions are about scheduling, not law. A few habits make visits easier for everyone:

- **Share your preferred notice method.** Tell the landlord in writing whether text or email reaches you fastest.
- **Offer time windows.** When a repair is needed, suggest two or three times that work for you.
- **Propose alternatives quickly.** If a proposed time does not work, reply right away in writing with other options.
- **Group visits.** Ask whether several repairs or an inspection can happen in one visit.
- **Say if you want to be home.** If you would like to be there, say so and offer times when you can be.
- **Prepare the space.** Secure pets, put away valuables, and clear a path to the area being worked on.
- **Note the results.** After each visit, write down what was done and anything left unfinished. Take a photo if something changed.
