# Is Small Claims Right for This Dispute?

> DRAFT — AI-drafted from sources/facts.yaml for attorney review. Not for release until approved.

**In this chapter.** Before you fill out any forms, it helps to check whether small claims is a good procedural fit for your dispute. This chapter walks through the dollar limits, the yearly two-claims rule, the rule against splitting a claim, time limits, and the extra step for claims against a government agency. It ends with a fit checklist you can work through on your own or with a free small claims advisor.

## Small claims is built around money

The small claims limits are written as dollar amounts [F:limit-natural] [F:limit-entity]. So the first question is simple: what amount of money are you asking for, and how did you arrive at it?

Write the number down. Next to it, list each part that makes up the total. For example: an unpaid invoice, a repair bill, a deposit that was not returned. Chapter 5 shows how to lay out this arithmetic clearly.

If what you want is not money, such as getting an item back or making someone stop doing something, check the California Courts self-help site to see what a small claims court can order [S:cc-selfhelp-small-claims]. This book does not cover that question.

## Check the dollar limits

- An individual (a "natural person") may generally sue for up to $12,500 [F:limit-natural].
- A plaintiff that is not a natural person, such as a corporation, LLC, or partnership, has a general limit of $6,250 [F:limit-entity].

These are general limits. Check the current statutes and the California Courts page to confirm which limit applies to your type of claim [S:ccp-116-220] [S:ccp-116-221].

## The two-claims rule

A person may not file more than two small claims cases asking for more than $2,500 anywhere in California in the same calendar year [F:two-claims-rule]. This counts cases filed in any California county, not just yours.

Your filing history also matters for fees. Filing fees depend on the amount claimed and how many small claims you have filed in the past 12 months [F:fees-vary]. Check your court's current fee schedule for the exact amount.

## Do not split one claim

You may not split one claim into several cases to fit under the limit [F:waive-excess]. And if you sue in small claims for less than your full amount, you generally give up the rest [F:waive-excess].

Here is what that means in practice. Say your total is $15,000 and you are an individual. You could not file two cases for $7,500 each. If you filed one case for $12,500, you would generally give up the other $2,500.

If your amount is above the limit, check the California Courts self-help site for information about other court options before you decide how to proceed [S:cc-selfhelp-small-claims]. This is also a point where some people choose to talk with a lawyer, which the rules allow before the hearing [F:no-attorneys].

## Time limits: issue-spotting only

A **statute of limitations** is a deadline set by law for filing a lawsuit. Here are the general periods for a few common kinds of claims:

| Kind of claim | General period |
|---|---|
| Breach of a written contract | 4 years [F:sol-written-contract] |
| Breach of an oral (spoken) contract | 2 years [F:sol-oral-contract] |
| Damage to property | 3 years [F:sol-property-damage] |
| Personal injury | 2 years [F:sol-personal-injury] |

Use this table to spot a possible issue, not to settle it. This book does not cover which category fits a dispute, when the clock starts, or whether any exception applies. Check the statutes themselves for those details [S:ccp-337] [S:ccp-339] [S:ccp-338] [S:ccp-335-1]. If your dates are close to one of these periods, note it as an open question. Free small claims advisor help is available in each county [F:advisors].

## Claims against a public entity

A **public entity** is a government body, such as a city, county, state agency, or school district. Suing one has an extra step.

Before suing a public entity, a person usually must first present a **government claim** to that entity [F:government-claim]. This is generally due within six months for injury or property damage, and within one year for other claims [F:government-claim]. You may sue only after the claim is denied, or is treated as denied [F:government-claim].

For how and where to present a government claim, and any deadline after a denial, check the official sources [S:gov-911-2] [S:gov-945-4]. These points are not covered in this book.

## When a different procedure may apply

This kit covers one thing: starting and pursuing a California small claims case as the plaintiff. Some disputes follow a different process. Examples include an eviction, a family law matter, responding to a case someone filed against you, or a case in another state or in federal court.

If your dispute looks like one of these, or you are not sure, check the California Courts self-help site to find the process that applies [S:cc-selfhelp-small-claims]. A small claims advisor can also help you find the right starting point [F:advisors].

> **LegalFriend practice note** — Before deciding anything, try writing the dispute in three or four plain sentences: who, what happened, when, and how much. If you cannot yet say when it happened or how you got the dollar amount, those are the gaps to fill first.

> **LegalFriend practice note** — Keep a simple log of any small claims cases you have filed, with the date, county, and amount. It makes the two-claims rule and the fee questions quick to answer.

## Checklist

- [ ] I can state the dollar amount I am asking for and list each part of it.
- [ ] My total is within the general limit for an individual or for a business, as it applies to me.
- [ ] I have confirmed the current limit on the official source.
- [ ] I am asking for my full amount in one case, not splitting it into several.
- [ ] I have counted my small claims cases over $2,500 filed this calendar year.
- [ ] I have written down the key dates: when the problem happened and when I found out.
- [ ] I have compared those dates with the general time limits and noted any that look close.
- [ ] I know whether the other side is a public entity. If so, I have looked into the government claim step.
- [ ] My dispute is not one that follows a different process (such as eviction or family law), or I have checked the official site.
- [ ] I have written a three-to-four sentence summary of the dispute.
