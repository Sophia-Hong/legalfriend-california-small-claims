# Identify the Right Defendant

> DRAFT — AI-drafted from sources/facts.yaml for attorney review. Not for release until approved.

**In this chapter.** The defendant is the person or business you are suing. This chapter explains why the exact legal name matters, how to find it using public records, and what to do when a business uses a different name or when there is more than one defendant. It also covers a form you may need if you run a business under an assumed name.

## Why the exact name matters

California Courts warns that naming the defendant incorrectly can prevent you from collecting after you receive a judgment in your favor [F:defendant-name-collection]. The official forms page puts special weight on the defendant's proper legal name [S:cc-selfhelp-fill-out-forms].

The name you use every day is not always the legal name. A landlord may go by a nickname. A shop may show one name on its sign and another on its receipts. The goal of this chapter is to find the name that appears in official records and keep proof of where you found it.

Do not guess. If you cannot confirm a name, write it down as an open question and keep looking. You can also ask a free small claims advisor for help [F:advisors].

## If the defendant is an individual

For a person, gather:

- Full first and last name, spelled exactly, with any middle name or initial you know.
- Any other names the person uses, such as a nickname or a former last name.
- A current address where the person lives or works.
- Where you found each piece of information, and the date.

Look at your own papers first. Leases, contracts, invoices, receipts, checks, and messages often show a full name. If two documents spell a name differently, note both.

How to list other names on SC-100 is explained on the official forms page [S:cc-selfhelp-fill-out-forms] [S:jcc-form-sc-100].

## If the defendant is a business

Businesses come in different forms. Common ones include:

- **Sole proprietorship** — one person owns and runs the business.
- **Partnership** — two or more people own the business together.
- **Corporation** ("Inc." or "Corp.") or **limited liability company** ("LLC") — a business registered with the state as its own entity.

The official forms page explains how to list different kinds of businesses on SC-100 [S:cc-selfhelp-fill-out-forms]. Check it before you fill in the defendant section, and note which kind of business you believe you are dealing with.

If the other side is a government body, such as a city or school district, see Chapter 2 about the government claim step [F:government-claim].

## How to look up a business

**California Secretary of State (bizfile Online).** The Secretary of State keeps an online search of registered corporations, LLCs, and some partnerships. Search for "bizfile Online" on the Secretary of State website. Then:

1. Search by the name you know.
2. Open the matching record. Note the exact name, including punctuation and endings like "Inc." or "LLC."
3. Note the entity number, the status shown, and the addresses listed.
4. Note the agent for service of process, if one is listed. This is the person or company named to receive legal papers for the business. Chapter 8 explains how service works [S:ccp-116-340].
5. Save or print the record and write the date on it.

If you find several similar names, compare the addresses and other details with your own documents. If nothing matches, note that. The business may be registered under another name, or it may be a sole proprietorship that is not in this database.

**County fictitious business name records.** Counties keep records of fictitious business names, usually through the county clerk or clerk-recorder's office. Many offer an online search; others take requests in person or by mail. These records often show who registered the name. That is the person or entity behind it.

## Fictitious business names

A **fictitious business name** (FBN, sometimes called a "DBA," for "doing business as") is a name a business uses that is different from its legal name. For example, a person named Maria Lopez might run "Sunrise Cleaning," or an LLC might operate a store under a brand name.

When you find an FBN, trace it back to the owner. The county record may name a person or an entity. If it names an entity, look that entity up on bizfile. Write down both the FBN and the owner's legal name.

## If you are the plaintiff and use a fictitious business name

This point is about you, not the defendant. A plaintiff doing business under a fictitious business name must file a declaration, form SC-103, showing compliance with the fictitious business name laws [F:fbn-declaration]. Check the current version on the official form page [S:jcc-form-sc-103].

## More than one defendant

A case starts with SC-100 [F:sc100-starts-case]. When SC-100 does not have room for all the defendants (or plaintiffs), form SC-100A is used to list the others [F:sc100a] [S:jcc-form-sc-100a].

Whether more than one party can be responsible for your claim is not covered in this book. Check the official forms page for how to list multiple defendants [S:cc-selfhelp-fill-out-forms]. California Courts also offers free form-filling help [F:guide-and-file].

> **LegalFriend practice note** — Make a one-page "name sheet" for each defendant. List the exact name, the type (person or kind of business), each address, and every source you checked with the date. Attach printouts or screenshots behind it. This keeps your research in one place when you fill out SC-100.

> **LegalFriend practice note** — Business records change. A quick re-check of bizfile and the county records shortly before you file helps confirm that nothing has changed since your first search.

## Checklist

- [ ] I know whether each defendant is a person, a business, or a government body.
- [ ] For each person: I have the full legal name, any other names, and a current address.
- [ ] For each business: I searched bizfile Online and saved the record with the date.
- [ ] I noted the exact name, entity number, status, addresses, and agent for service shown.
- [ ] I checked county fictitious business name records for any name that might be an FBN.
- [ ] I traced every FBN back to the legal owner's name.
- [ ] I read the official forms page on how to list each kind of defendant.
- [ ] If I use a fictitious business name, I have looked at form SC-103.
- [ ] If there are more defendants than fit on SC-100, I have looked at form SC-100A.
- [ ] I made a name sheet for each defendant and listed any open questions.
