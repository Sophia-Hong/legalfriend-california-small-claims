# What California Small Claims Is

> DRAFT — AI-drafted from sources/facts.yaml for attorney review. Not for release until approved.

**In this chapter.** You will get a plain-English picture of California small claims court: what it is, how much you can ask for, and who uses it. You will also see how the official California Courts website breaks the process into four steps, and where to find free help along the way.

## What small claims court is

Small claims is a simpler court process for smaller money disputes. People usually handle their own cases. The rules about who can sue, for how much, and how the hearing works come from California law. The California Courts self-help website explains them in plain language [S:cc-selfhelp-small-claims].

A few words will come up again and again in this book:

- **Plaintiff** — the person or business that starts the case. This book is written for plaintiffs.
- **Defendant** — the person, business, or other party being sued.
- **Claim** — what the plaintiff is asking the court for. In this book, that means a dollar amount.
- **Judgment** — the court's final decision in the case.

A small claims case starts with a form called SC-100, Plaintiff's Claim and ORDER to Go to Small Claims Court [F:sc100-starts-case]. As observed on September 28, 2026, the California Courts SC-100 page listed the form as effective January 1, 2026 [F:sc100-effective]. Forms change. Open the current form page before you fill anything out [S:jcc-form-sc-100].

## Current dollar limits

Small claims has limits on how much you can ask for.

- An individual (the law calls this a "natural person") may generally sue for up to $12,500 [F:limit-natural].
- A plaintiff that is not a natural person, such as a corporation, LLC, or partnership, has a general limit of $6,250 [F:limit-entity].
- A person may not file more than two small claims cases asking for more than $2,500 anywhere in California in the same calendar year [F:two-claims-rule].

These are general limits. Check the current statutes and the California Courts page to confirm whether any special rule applies to your type of claim [S:ccp-116-220] [S:ccp-116-221]. Chapter 2 looks at the limits in more detail.

## Who uses small claims

Both individuals and businesses file small claims cases. Common examples are everyday money disputes between tenants and landlords, customers and businesses, or neighbors. Whether a particular dispute fits small claims is the subject of Chapter 2.

A business that files as the plaintiff has its own rules. A corporation or other entity appears through an authorized employee, officer, or director, not an attorney [F:entity-appearance]. It also has the lower dollar limit described above [F:limit-entity].

## No lawyers at the hearing

In small claims, parties generally may not be represented by an attorney at the hearing [F:no-attorneys]. You may still talk to a lawyer before or after the hearing [F:no-attorneys]. In practice, this means you will likely explain your own side to the judge. Later chapters cover how to organize your papers and prepare a short, clear summary.

## Free help from the courts

- **Small claims advisors.** Each county provides free small claims advisor help [F:advisors]. To find your county's advisor and hours, check your court's website or the California Courts self-help site [S:cc-selfhelp-small-claims].
- **Free form help.** California Courts offers free help filling out forms, including a tool called Guide & File [F:guide-and-file].
- **Fees and fee waivers.** Filing fees depend on how much you are asking for and how many small claims you have filed in the past 12 months [F:fees-vary]. Check your court's current fee schedule rather than relying on a number you read elsewhere. If you cannot afford court fees, you can ask for a fee waiver using form FW-001 [F:fee-waiver] [S:jcc-form-fw-001].

## The official four-step process

The California Courts self-help website organizes small claims into four steps: **Before you start**, **Start a case**, **Go to your court date**, and **After the trial** [S:cc-selfhelp-small-claims]. Here is how this book lines up with those steps. For the exact contents of each official step, check the official site itself.

**1. Before you start.** Here you look at whether small claims fits your dispute (Chapter 2), identify the right defendant (Chapter 3), and learn where you can file (Chapter 4). Where you file follows specific venue rules. Common examples are where a defendant lives or does business, or where a contract was made or was to be performed [F:venue-basics]. SC-100 also asks whether you asked the defendant to pay before filing, or why not [F:demand-first]. Chapter 5 covers that question.

**2. Start a case.** You fill out SC-100 (Chapter 6) and file it with the court (Chapter 7). Some courts require additional local forms, so check your court's website or clerk [F:local-forms] [S:cc-selfhelp-fill-out-forms]. The defendant must then be formally given the court papers. This is called "service." You may not serve the papers yourself [F:service-who]. Chapter 8 explains who can.

**3. Go to your court date.** You gather your papers and evidence and prepare a short explanation of what happened (Chapters 9 and 10).

**4. After the trial.** The clerk sends the parties a Notice of Entry of Judgment (form SC-130) [F:notice-of-entry]. A plaintiff cannot appeal a judgment on the plaintiff's own claim [F:plaintiff-no-appeal]. Chapter 11 gives an overview of what comes next, including payment and collection.

> **LegalFriend practice note** — Official pages and forms are updated from time to time. When you print or save a court page or form, write the date you viewed it on the first page. If two versions ever differ, that date shows which one you used.

> **LegalFriend practice note** — Many people find it helpful to keep one folder, paper or digital, for the whole matter from day one. Put every letter, receipt, photo, and court paper in it as you go, in date order.
