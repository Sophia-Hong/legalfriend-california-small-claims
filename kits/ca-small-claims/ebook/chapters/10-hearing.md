# Prepare for the Hearing

> DRAFT — AI-drafted from sources/facts.yaml for attorney review. Not for release until approved.

**In this chapter:** The hearing is where you explain your claim to the judge and show your evidence. This chapter covers how to prepare a short, factual summary, put your papers in a clear order, and think through questions that may come up. It also covers who can speak at the hearing, what happens if the other side files its own claim, and how to ask for a new date.

## Who speaks at the hearing

In small claims court, you generally present your own case. Parties generally may not have a lawyer represent them at the hearing, but they may talk to a lawyer before or after it [F:no-attorneys]. If a party is a corporation or other business entity, it appears through an authorized employee, officer, or director rather than a lawyer [F:entity-appearance].

You do not have to prepare alone. Each county provides free small claims advisor help [F:advisors]. To find the advisor for your county, check the official source [S:cc-selfhelp-small-claims].

## Write a two-minute summary

A short summary helps you stay on track if you feel nervous. Aim for something you can say calmly in about two minutes. Many people find this structure useful:

1. **Who you are and who the other party is.** One sentence each.
2. **What the agreement or situation was.** For example, what was promised, bought, rented, or repaired.
3. **What happened.** The key events in date order. Leave out side stories.
4. **The amount you are asking for.** Say the total, then how you added it up.
5. **What you have to show it.** Name your main documents or photos.

Write it out, then read it aloud a few times. Cut any sentence that does not help explain the facts or the amount. Stick to facts you can support with a document, a photo, or your own direct knowledge. Describe the other party's actions plainly, without labels or guesses about motives.

> **LegalFriend practice note** — A common pitfall is opening with background that feels important but does not explain the amount being requested. If a sentence does not connect to what happened or to the total, consider moving it to an "only if asked" list.

## Put your evidence in order

Organize your evidence in the same order as your summary. That way, when you mention a document, you can find it right away.

- **Label each item.** Use a simple system, such as Exhibit 1, Exhibit 2, and so on. Put a small label or tab on each page or photo.
- **Make an index.** A one-page list of each exhibit, with a short description and date, is easy to follow.
- **Group by topic or date.** For example: the agreement, then payments, then messages, then photos, then estimates or receipts.
- **Prepare copies.** Plan for a set for yourself and extra sets for others. For how many copies your court expects and how it handles exhibits, check your court's website or the official source [S:cc-selfhelp-small-claims].
- **Print digital items.** Text messages, emails, and online records are easier to review on paper. Include the date, time, and sender where you can.
- **Build a damages table.** "Damages" means the money you are asking for. List each part of your total, the amount, and the exhibit that supports it. Show the math.

If a witness is coming with you, make sure they know the date, time, and place. Write down, in a sentence or two, what each witness saw or knows.

## Questions a judge may ask

Each judge runs a hearing in their own way. The general questions below come up in many kinds of disputes. Preparing short, honest answers can help you stay focused:

- What was the agreement, and was it written or spoken?
- When did the key events happen?
- How did you arrive at the amount you are asking for?
- Which documents or photos support that amount?
- Did you ask the other party to pay before you filed? What was the response?
- Has the other party paid or offered anything?
- Is there anything in the other party's account that you would like to respond to?

If you do not know an answer, it is fine to say so. Avoid guessing.

## If the defendant files a claim against you

The defendant may file a claim against you, using form SC-120, to be heard in the same case [F:defendant-claim]. If you receive one, read it carefully. Gather any documents that relate to it and add them to your exhibit index. For timing rules and what to do next, check the form page and the official source [S:jcc-form-sc-120] [S:cc-selfhelp-small-claims].

## If you need a new hearing date

A party can ask to postpone the hearing using form SC-150. Whether the request is granted is up to the court [F:postpone]. For deadlines, any fee, and how to submit the request, check the form page and your court [S:jcc-form-sc-150].

## Interpreters and accommodations

If you or a witness needs a language interpreter, or you need a disability accommodation, check the official source for how and when to ask [S:cc-selfhelp-small-claims]. Your court's website or clerk can explain the local process.

## What to bring

- The papers you filed, including your copy of the claim and the hearing date and location.
- A copy of the proof of service. After service, the server completes a proof of service (form SC-104), which is filed with the court before the hearing by the deadline the court specifies [F:proof-of-service].
- Your two-minute summary and your "only if asked" list.
- Your exhibit index and all labeled exhibits, plus copies.
- Your damages table.
- Any defendant's claim you received, and your documents about it.
- Names and contact details for any witnesses.
- A photo ID, a pen, and paper for notes.

> **LegalFriend practice note** — Plan to arrive early. Security lines and finding the right courtroom can take longer than expected. Waiting time is often better spent rereading the summary than reorganizing papers.

## Checklist

- [ ] I wrote a two-minute summary and practiced it aloud.

- [ ] I checked how many copies my court expects [S:cc-selfhelp-small-claims].

- [ ] I confirmed the proof of service was filed by the court's deadline [F:proof-of-service].

- [ ] If I or a witness needs an interpreter or accommodation, I checked the official process.
