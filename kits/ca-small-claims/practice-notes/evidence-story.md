# Telling a clear evidence story

> DRAFT — AI-drafted for attorney review. Not for release until approved.

In small claims, parties generally may not be represented by an attorney at the hearing, though they may consult one before or after. [F:no-attorneys] [S:ccp-116-530] That means you usually present your own papers and explain them yourself. This note shares general ways to organize that material so it is easy to follow. It does not say what evidence a court will accept or how any case will turn out.

## Chronology first

Start with a simple timeline before you sort any exhibits. An "exhibit" is a document, photo, or other item you show the judge.

- One line per event: date, what happened, and who was involved.
- Use plain, factual wording. Leave out opinions about the other side.
- Mark which events have a document or photo behind them.

The timeline becomes the spine of your story. Everything else hangs off it.

## One exhibit per point

It often helps when each exhibit shows one thing. A contract shows the deal. A receipt shows payment. A photo shows condition on a date. When one item tries to show everything, the key detail gets lost.

If you asked the other side to pay before filing, keep a copy of that request. The plaintiff's claim must state whether the plaintiff asked the defendant to pay before filing (or why not). [F:demand-first] [S:jcc-form-sc-100]

## Labeling

- Number exhibits in timeline order (Exhibit 1, 2, 3...).
- Put the number on the front of each page or the back of each photo.
- Write the date and a short caption on each photo, such as "Kitchen wall, move-out day."
- For long email or text threads, highlight only the lines that matter and keep the full thread behind it.

## Summary sheet

A one-page summary helps you stay on track when you are nervous. Include:

- Two or three sentences on what happened.
- The amount you are asking for.
- A short list of exhibits by number, with one line each on what it shows.

Keep it to one page. If it grows, cut detail, not exhibits.

## Damages table

"Damages" is the money you are asking for. A simple table makes the math easy to check:

| Item | Amount | Exhibit |
|---|---|---|
| Example: repair invoice | $___ | 4 |
| Example: replacement cost | $___ | 5 |
| **Total** | **$___** | |

It helps when each row points to a document. Check that the total matches what you wrote on your claim form.

## Witnesses

A witness is someone who saw or knows something firsthand. Write a short list of who they are and what each can speak to. Check the official guidance on witnesses, written statements, and how to ask someone to come to the hearing. [S:cc-selfhelp-small-claims]

## Keep it short and neutral

Judges often have many cases in one session. A calm, short, factual story is easier to follow than a long one. Describe what happened and what it cost. Avoid personal attacks, even if you feel strongly.

Also be ready for the other side's view. The defendant may file a claim against the plaintiff (form SC-120) to be heard in the same case. [F:defendant-claim] [S:jcc-form-sc-120]

## Copies and local practice

Courts can differ on how many copies to bring and how to hand up papers. Some courts require additional local forms, so check the specific court's website or clerk. [F:local-forms] Each county provides free small claims advisor assistance, and an advisor can help you check your organization. [F:advisors]

## Common pitfalls

- Bringing a stack of unsorted papers with no timeline.
- Photos with no date or caption.
- Exhibits that do not match the numbers on the summary sheet.
- A damages total that differs from the amount on the claim form.
- Long printouts of message threads with the key lines buried.
- Opinions and labels about the other side in place of facts.
- Only one copy of each exhibit, with nothing to share.
