# Before Filing

> DRAFT — AI-drafted from sources/facts.yaml for attorney review. Not for release until approved.

**In this chapter:** Much of the work in a small claims case happens before any form is filed. This chapter covers asking for payment first and keeping a record of it, saving evidence, building a dated timeline, and adding up the amount in a clear table. It ends with a reminder about time limits.

## Asking for payment first

The plaintiff's claim form has to say whether the plaintiff asked the defendant to pay before filing, or explain why not. Form SC-100 asks this question. [F:demand-first] [S:jcc-form-sc-100] The exact wording of that requirement is in the statute. Check the current text on the official source. [S:ccp-116-320]

A request for payment is often called a "demand." It can be a letter, an email, or another written message. A written request is easier to prove later than a phone call.

A clear, calm request usually covers:

- Who you are and who you are writing to.
- A short, factual description of what happened, with dates.
- The amount you are asking for and how you reached it (see the table below).
- How and where payment can be sent.
- A date by which you would like a reply.

Keep the tone neutral. Stick to facts and numbers. Avoid guesses about the other person's motives.

**Documenting the request.** Keep a copy of exactly what you sent. Note the date, the method (mail, email, text, hand delivery), and the address or account used. If you mail it, keep any mailing receipt. Save any reply, including a reply that says no. If you did not ask for payment, write down the reason while it is fresh, since the form asks about it. [F:demand-first]

> **LegalFriend practice note** — A request for payment is also a good moment to double-check your own numbers. People often find a math error or a missing receipt while writing it. Fixing that before filing keeps the claim form and the request consistent.

## Saving evidence

Evidence is anything that helps show what happened and how much money is involved. Start collecting it now, while it is easy to find.

- **Papers:** contracts, invoices, receipts, estimates, bank or card statements, canceled checks, leases, and notices.
- **Messages:** emails, texts, and app or social media messages. Save them with the date, time, and sender visible.
- **Photos and video:** keep the original files. Original files often carry the date they were taken.
- **Witnesses:** write down the name and contact details of anyone who saw what happened.

Some simple habits help:

- Keep originals unchanged. Work from copies.
- Back up digital files in a second place.
- Give each item a short label and date, such as "2026-03-14 invoice from repair shop."
- Keep everything, including items that do not seem to help. A complete file makes it easier to understand the whole story.

## Building a dated timeline

A timeline (also called a chronology) is a list of events in date order. It helps you tell the story clearly and spot gaps.

Use three columns:

| Date | What happened | Proof |
|---|---|---|
| 2026-02-01 | Signed written agreement for the work | Agreement, page 1–2 |
| 2026-02-20 | Paid deposit | Bank statement, line 4 |
| 2026-03-14 | Work reported as finished | Text message, photo |
| 2026-04-02 | Sent written request for payment | Copy of letter, mailing receipt |

*These rows are made-up examples to show the format.*

Tips: use exact dates when you have them. When you do not, write "about" and explain why. Link each event to the item that proves it. If an event has no proof, mark it so you know it is a gap.

## Adding up the amount

SC-100 includes questions about the amount being claimed and how it was figured. Follow the current form's own instructions for the exact items. [S:jcc-form-sc-100] A simple itemized table makes the math easy to follow and check.

| # | Item | Amount | Proof |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Amount paid under the agreement | $1,800.00 | Receipt, 2026-02-20 |
| 2 | Cost of replacement part | $245.50 | Store receipt, 2026-03-20 |
| 3 | Partial refund already received | −$400.00 | Bank statement, 2026-04-10 |
| | **Total** | **$1,645.50** | |

*Made-up numbers, for format only.*

Show the arithmetic in writing:

- Step 1: $1,800.00 + $245.50 = $2,045.50
- Step 2: $2,045.50 − $400.00 = $1,645.50

Subtract any money already paid back to you, so the total reflects what is still unpaid. Keep one row per item, with its own proof.

This table is only arithmetic. Whether a particular item can be recovered is a legal question this kit does not answer. Check the official self-help guidance or ask a small claims advisor. [S:cc-selfhelp-small-claims] Each county provides free small claims advisor help. [F:advisors] [S:ccp-116-260]

**Compare the total to the limits.** A natural person (an individual) may generally sue for up to $12,500 in small claims. [F:limit-natural] [S:ccp-116-221] The general limit for plaintiffs that are not natural persons, such as corporations, LLCs, and partnerships, is $6,250. [F:limit-entity] [S:ccp-116-220] A claim may not be split into several cases to fit under the limit. A plaintiff who sues in small claims for less than the full amount generally gives up the rest. [F:waive-excess] If your total is near or above a limit, check the current rule on the official source before filing. [S:ccp-116-220]

## A reminder about time limits

Claims have deadlines, called "limitations periods" (or statutes of limitations). These are the general periods listed in this kit:

- Breach of a written contract: four years. [F:sol-written-contract] [S:ccp-337]
- Breach of an oral contract: two years. [F:sol-oral-contract] [S:ccp-339]
- Damage to property: three years. [F:sol-property-damage] [S:ccp-338]
- Personal injury: two years. [F:sol-personal-injury] [S:ccp-335-1]

These are general periods only. This kit does not explain when a period starts or what might change it. Check the current rule on the official source.

**Claims against a public entity.** Before suing a public entity, such as a city, county, state agency, or school district, a claimant usually must first present a government claim. This is generally within six months for injury or property damage and one year for other claims. The claimant may sue only after the claim is denied or deemed denied. [F:government-claim] [S:gov-911-2] [S:gov-945-4]

> **LegalFriend practice note** — Put the key dates from your timeline next to these periods on one page. Seeing them side by side makes it easier to notice a question that needs checking early, rather than close to a deadline.

## Checklist

- [ ] I asked the other side for payment in writing, or wrote down why I did not.
- [ ] I kept a copy of my request, the date, and how I sent it.
- [ ] I saved any reply.
- [ ] I gathered papers, messages, photos, and witness details.
- [ ] I kept originals unchanged and backed up digital files.
- [ ] I built a dated timeline linking each event to its proof.
- [ ] I made an itemized table of the amount, with the arithmetic shown.
- [ ] I subtracted any money already paid back.
- [ ] I compared my total to the current limit that applies to me.
- [ ] I checked the general time limits and noted any questions.
- [ ] If the other side is a public entity, I checked the government claim rules.
- [ ] I listed open questions to ask a small claims advisor.
