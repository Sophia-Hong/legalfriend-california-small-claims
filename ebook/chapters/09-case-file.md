# Build the Case File

> DRAFT — AI-drafted from sources/facts.yaml for attorney review. Not for release until approved.

**In this chapter:** A case file is one organized collection of everything about your dispute. This chapter shows how to list your documents, build a timeline, label exhibits, connect each fact to the evidence behind it, record witness information, and document your damages. Most of this chapter is practical organization, not legal rules.

## Why a case file matters

Parties generally may not be represented by an attorney at the small claims hearing, though they may consult one before or after [F:no-attorneys]. A business or other entity appears through an authorized employee, officer, or director rather than an attorney [F:entity-appearance]. That means you, or your business's representative, will explain the dispute yourself. A well-organized file makes it easier to find the right page when you need it.

This chapter does not tell you which evidence matters most in your dispute. It gives you a way to organize what you have so you can see it clearly.

## Step 1: Document inventory

Start by gathering every document and item connected to the dispute in one place. Then make a list. An inventory is simply a numbered list of what you have.

Common items include:

- Contracts, leases, invoices, estimates, and receipts.
- Letters, emails, and text messages.
- Photos and videos, with the date each was taken.
- Bank or card statements showing payments.
- Your written request for payment, if you made one. SC-100 asks whether you asked the defendant to pay before filing, or why not [F:demand-first]. Keep a copy of that request and any reply.
- Your filed SC-100, the filed proof of service (SC-104) [F:proof-of-service], and anything else the court sent you.
- If the defendant files a claim against you on form SC-120 to be heard in the same case [F:defendant-claim], keep those papers in the file as well.

For each item, write down: a short description, the date, who created or sent it, and where the original is kept.

> **LegalFriend practice note** — Do not throw anything away while you sort, even items that seem unhelpful. Keep originals safe and work from copies. For messages on a phone, save screenshots that show the sender, the date, and the full message.

## Step 2: Build a timeline

A timeline is a list of events in date order. It helps you, and anyone listening, follow what happened.

Use a simple table with four columns:

| Date | What happened | Who was involved | Evidence (exhibit no.) |
|---|---|---|---|
| | | | |

Tips for a clear timeline:

- One event per row.
- Use exact dates when you know them. If you only know the month, say so.
- Describe what happened in plain, neutral words. Stick to facts you can support.
- Leave the evidence column blank when you have no document yet. Those blanks show you what is missing.

Some rules depend on specific dates. For example, in a residential security deposit dispute, a landlord must return the deposit, or an itemized statement of deductions with any remaining balance, within 21 days after the tenant moves out [F:deposit-21-days]. In a dispute like that, the move-out date and the date anything was received belong on the timeline.

## Step 3: Label exhibits and map facts to evidence

An exhibit is a document or item you plan to show the court. Labeling makes each one easy to find and refer to.

A simple labeling method:

1. Put your exhibits in the same order as your timeline.
2. Give each one a number or letter (Exhibit 1, Exhibit 2, and so on).
3. Write the label on a small sticker or in the corner of the first page.
4. Number the pages inside longer exhibits.
5. Make an exhibit list: label, short description, date, number of pages.

Then map each fact to its evidence. Take every important statement in your timeline and write which exhibit supports it. For example: "Paid $X on [date] — Exhibit 3, bank statement, page 2." If a fact has no exhibit, mark it. You may have a witness who can speak to it, or it may be something to note as unsupported.

How many copies of exhibits to bring, and how your court handles photos, videos, or electronic files, can vary. Check the official guidance and your court's instructions [S:cc-selfhelp-small-claims].

> **LegalFriend practice note** — A one-page exhibit list at the front of your file is easy to scan quickly. Many people prepare separate, identical sets: one for themselves and extra sets in case they are needed. Confirm with your court what it expects.

## Step 4: Witness information

A witness is a person who saw, heard, or knows something about the dispute firsthand. For each possible witness, record:

- Full name and contact information.
- What they saw or know, in a sentence or two.
- Which facts on your timeline they relate to.
- Whether they are willing and able to come to the hearing on your date.

Whether a witness can give a written statement instead of appearing, and how to require a witness to attend, are covered by official procedures and forms. This kit does not state those rules. Check the official guidance and the small claims forms list [S:cc-selfhelp-small-claims] [S:cc-small-claims-forms].

## Step 5: Damages proof

"Damages" means the money amount you are asking for. A damages table in your case file shows how you reached that number.

Build a damages table:

| Item | Amount | How calculated | Exhibit |
|---|---|---|---|
| | | | |
| **Total** | | | |

- List each part of the amount on its own row.
- Show the math. For example, "3 months × monthly amount."
- Link each row to a receipt, invoice, estimate, or statement.
- Check that your total matches the amount on your SC-100 [S:jcc-form-sc-100].

If a number is an estimate, label it as one and keep the source of the estimate.

## Step 6: Organize your copies

Put everything into one binder or folder, in this order:

1. Your short timeline.
2. Exhibit list.
3. Exhibits, in labeled order.
4. Damages table.
5. Witness list.
6. Court papers (SC-100, proof of service, and anything the court sent).

Keep a digital copy as a backup. Store originals separately from the copies you work from.

Free help is available as you organize. Each county provides free small claims advisor assistance [F:advisors].
